Cannabis enjoys a reputation for being a sort of cuddly, easy-to-treat-casually drug among its users and non-users alike. It is indebted to the fact that many go through a phase of vape-hits, bong-rips, and chewable gummies throughout their teens and twenties, only to moderate or abandon the drug altogether at the onset of life’s real responsibilities. This is undeserved. For me and many others, it is a substance that is prone to abuse and catastrophic psychological fallout. Its embrace is acidic: warm at first, but damn if it isn’t painful after a while.

It is because of cannabis that I have had two multi-week stays in psych wards, believing everyone there was plotting to vivisect me. I have torn up the linoleum floors of one of my psych ward rooms, looking for hidden messages underneath. I have thought things so alien and disturbing, so non-native to my mind, that I came to believe that my COVID-19 vaccination also contained microscopic machines that invaded and rewired my brain. I have lied repeatedly to those closest to me, damaging our relationships in ways that I am still repairing. I have become an other to myself.

Please take this testimony as it's intended: to convince you of the dangers of cannabis abuse, without going so far as to recommend total prohibition of the substance. To be sure, I have problems with the way the market has currently been flooded with gas-station-legal THC derivatives of all kinds. But prohibition requires the prosecution, sentencing, and imprisoning of people for the crime of peacefully altering their own consciousnesses, something which is in my view unsupportable. This leaves me in the uncomfortable position of wishing it were federally legal while being terrified of the consequences that exposure to market dynamics and the sunsetting of its taboo will have for the psychologically vulnerable. One wishes that education and mental health screening were enough to address the cannabis question, but everyone must be familiar with the ease with which warning signs can be ignored in the pursuit of sensory gratification. I certainly am. Be careful; it can sneak up on you.

But so my story goes on. After dealing with the consequences of THC abuse more than once and coming out the other side each time more convinced of the necessity of sobriety from my substance of choice, I have come to a few conclusions that have shaken my atheistic worldview and that could serve others in a world of rising rates of cannabis use. I owe to my episodes of despair both a new desire for a more deliberate moral framework than could be provided by mere cultural osmosis and a new understanding of the reason-enhancing and anxiolytic effects of prayer and meditation.

Had you asked at nearly any point during the past fifteen years if I were religious, I would have answered resolutely in the negative; being an atheist and an anti-theist was core to my identity, and I would not have let the opportunity pass to expound on various arguments against God and religion, arguments which were first made by better atheists than me and which still are, even now, dear to my heart. Even during my experimental sojourns in the beautiful and chaotic worlds of LSD and psilocybin — where patterns emerge everywhere and interweave and dance and breathe, and where one becomes overwhelmed by the beauty and meaning in ordinary, small-in-the-grand-scheme things — experiences of the numinous were rare, and even at their heights nothing was felt beyond a sense of pure marvel at the hallucinations, the time-warps, and the intense appreciation for art of all kinds that these drugs reliably produce.

I can't say that atheism was the cause of my pursuit of cannabis over everything — including core values like honesty and integrity — simply because of the fact that there are many atheists I have known who behave in an ethically exceptional manner. But I can say that in the aftermath of my mental detonation I have discovered slowly and not without pain that I am a more religious person, albeit one who disbelieves the miracles and the Resurrection and who cannot bring himself not to ultimately be agnostic as to whether God exists, anyway, the question seeming to me to be kind of moot, with God thankfully having not revealed Himself and with all of religion’s positive effects seeming to operate without any such revelation being necessary. Prayer and the story of Christ have become important. Meditation and the Middle Path have become important. And these things’ becoming important has brought about a turning within me from an atheist worldview to a more-so-but-not-quite-fully-religious one, on the basis of which I feel utterly comfortable with calling myself both Christian and Buddhist, despite the apparent contradiction and even if an ultimate, literal belief proves impossible.

This turn I have taken toward religion was not the result of any reevaluation of scriptural claims to historicity. It remains obvious that any truth once represented by our religious texts has long since been obscured by mutation and embellishment, a process that owes its drive to man’s tendency to manipulate the truth, whether consciously or not. Our religions are full of lies. The miracles and the Resurrection did not occur. There is no such thing as spiritual enlightenment.

But these are useful lies. It is precisely our tendency to manipulate the truth that has delivered to us a set of world religions whose beliefs and practices have such a high degree of psychological potency. People repeat those aspects of a story or practice that are most gripping and effective, and this happens iteratively, generation after generation, the result of which is an evolutionary process that appears to be aiming for salience in the same way that biological evolution appears to be aiming for fitness. From this we can conclude that our religious traditions contain concepts and practices that are psychologically salient to the highest degree.

This line of thinking became meaningful to me only after I was forced to abandon pure atheism and start to flirt with what religion has to offer. Upon experiencing traumatic drug addiction and withdrawals, I had to stop believing that ultimate truth was necessary for a belief or practice to be useful. As I suffered through psychotic withdrawals, the positive effects of prayer and meditation became undeniable.

I failed in confrontation with the multiplicity of outcomes that arise from any given moral decision, and I am now aided by having a dialog with a hypothetical higher power. From each moral decision stems a cascade of consequences, each requiring yet more decisions and yielding yet more consequences, and so on, the final results of which we can be only dimly aware. The good atheist has no problem choosing the actions that beget the best of outcomes. However, I as a pure atheist did not get the best outcomes — not by any means.

Prayer is now essential for me in navigating the problem of outcome multiplicity. When you sit down and ask a God, even a hypothetical God, what you should do in any moment of genuine uncertainty, you'd be surprised at the quality and thoughtfulness of the answer you get. To be clear, I don't believe that the answer comes from outside my own mind when I do this. I simply believe that by making moral reasoning linguistic and therefore explicit, a lens of clarity is applied to an otherwise blurry moral picture. And this clarity is essential to navigating the complexity of the outcome cascade that daily life presents to us.
